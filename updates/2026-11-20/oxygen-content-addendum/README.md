# Oxygen in carrier fluids across pressures and formulations

The Stage's first milestone is artificial blood. To compare candidate fluids, we need to know how much oxygen a measured volume of finished fluid carries at more than one pressure. This addendum starts with an animal study that measured oxygen on both sides of a tissue bed. Further sections test archival emulsions, hemoglobin analyzers, a gas chamber and engineered cardiac tissue, and explain a mistake in our first reading of an emulsion table. The observations come from the original laboratories. [First release and replay](../oxygen-measurements/README.md)

## A regional blood-flow measurement separates oxygen delivery from uptake

Standl and colleagues measured regional blood flow and assayed oxygen content directly in arterial and popliteal venous blood. Six dogs received HBOC-201 and six received stored red cells. The same study recorded blood-gas partial pressures. At the final observation, the mean arterial-to-venous pressure gradients were close, **79 and 82 mmHg**, while the measured contents gave mean arterial-to-venous differences of **3.7 and 1.5 mL O₂/dL**. Here and below the order is HBOC-201, then red cells. The result supplies an observable missing from a pressure-only assessment of oxygen supply to tissue. [Standl and colleagues, measurements and Tables I–III](https://doi.org/10.1067/mva.2003.127)

**The authors' calculated regional rates.** For each animal, flow multiplied by arterial oxygen content gives incoming oxygen delivery. Flow multiplied by the arterial-to-venous content difference gives net uptake. The authors reported final group mean delivery of **6.7 and 10.0 mL O₂/min** and mean net uptake of **3.5 and 1.7 mL O₂/min**. In the sampled muscle region the HBOC group’s mean incoming delivery was lower and its mean net uptake was higher.

**An independent check on the six-animal summaries.** We computed limits using only the group means and standard errors of flow, arterial content, and the within-animal content difference. For six animals, the difference between the average of paired products and the product of the averages is bounded by five times the product of the two standard errors. We allowed arbitrary correlation within each group and half a final printed digit of rounding in every mean and standard error. The HBOC group's mean net uptake still exceeded the red-cell group's by **at least 0.8955 mL/min**. The red-cell group’s mean delivery exceeded the HBOC group's by **at least 1.6875 mL/min**. The bounds assume a valid oxygen-content calibration in both groups. They cover sample correlation and printed rounding. A material-dependent assay bias could alter the comparison, and these group summaries cannot establish a carrier-specific causal effect. The bounds apply to these sampled groups. Human transfusion outcomes remain outside this study. [Replay the bounds or verify the inputs against the source PDF](standl/paired_content.py)

**Why the within-animal measurements matter.** We repeated the calculation using the separate arterial and venous summaries and withheld the reported summary of their matched difference. The conservative lower bound on the HBOC-minus-red-cell uptake gap was **−0.163 mL/min** from the separate summaries. Adding the measured mean arterial-to-venous difference tightened the bound to **−0.057**. Adding its reported standard error brought it to **+0.8955 mL/min**. The spread of the paired differences made the sign identifiable under this calibration. Two hypothetical six-animal groups in each arm can match the retained means and standard errors within printed precision while giving opposite gaps, **−0.1551 or +3.74 mL/min**. The examples use nonnegative values and are mathematical reconstructions. The historical study’s matched-difference summary rules out the reversal. [Replay Lilitari’s paired-data check using exact arithmetic](standl/pairing_ablation.py)

## How well did a rule from one study predict venous oxygen in another?

Using Standl’s data for calibration, we froze eight candidate rules for predicting the mean oxygen content in blood leaving the limb in Freitag and colleagues’ later study. A simple rule carried forward the median fraction of arterial oxygen extracted in the corresponding experimental phase. Across the six post-baseline group means it had a **0.676 mL O₂/dL** mean absolute error and **0.756 mL O₂/dL** root mean square error. The six values are three repeat occasions in each of two groups of six dogs. The later study involved some of the same investigators. Two carrier-exposure conditions went beyond the training ranges of arterial content and flow. [Freitag and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03022050) · [Eight candidate rules and results](freitag/RESULTS.md)

The treated baseline gives arterial content 22.0 and venous content 13.1 mL/dL, while the printed arterial-to-venous difference is 5.1. The first two means differ by 8.9. We kept the original readings and reported baseline performance separately. We also found that the printed baseline delivery falls outside the flow–content product bounds allowed by the reported means, standard deviations and rounding. The coordinator had seen the target table. The modeling programs received no target venous values. During extraction, the two baseline venous means were seen after rules were fixed and before the numerical forecast file was locked. These conditions define the retrospective test. [Sources and replay from both original PDFs](freitag/SOURCE.md)

Four additional forecasting programs were evaluated on the same outcomes. One selected a constant-uptake rule by comparing errors when an entire training group was withheld. It gave the smallest observed error among the twelve candidates, **0.499 mL O₂/dL mean absolute error** and **0.549 mL O₂/dL root mean square error**. That ranking was made after scoring. Even the best-fitting constant uptake required at least **0.864 mL O₂/dL** extra tolerance to reconcile the *training* observations with a common value of **2.723 mL O₂/min**. Its lower test error therefore supports its role as a working predictor for these occasions. Its fixed-uptake mechanism failed the original, unrelaxed training constraints. After the main replay, `python minimum_slack.py` reproduces these values from the source-derived data. [Exact-arithmetic check](freitag/minimum_slack.py)

## What Navari and colleagues measured in each emulsion

Our blind forecast used the wrong outcome. We treated the entries for FC-47 emulsions as pressure-normalized coefficients. The data compiler's note about a possible Bunsen coefficient applies to the separate, graph-derived entries for neat liquid. The emulsion rows report a volume of oxygen per volume of the finished dispersion **at each stated equilibration pressure**. This error invalidates our registered comparison as a physical test. The individual pressure forecasts and scores were fixed before we saw the target means. The correction and the interpolation below came after disclosure. [Navari and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01851388) · [Compiler's table, printed p. 386](https://iupac.github.io/SolubilityDataSeries/volumes/SDS-7.pdf#page=403)

In the 22 g/100 mL emulsion at 310.15 K, the tabulated means at 50 and 760 mmHg supplied endpoints for a straight-line interpolation. Its mean absolute error at 100 and 150 mmHg was **0.0003465 cm³ oxygen per cm³ finished fluid**. A line drawn through zero and the 760-mmHg observation gave **0.0014586**. The gain survives perturbations of half a printed unit. Allowing each measurement to move by one reported standard deviation permits the ranking to reverse. Each mean represents five determinations. The gas-volume reference needed for an absolute molar conversion remains unresolved. [Calculations, observations and sensitivity bounds](navari/README.md)

## Can a correction transfer between the carriers studied by Freire and colleagues?

Freire and colleagues reported three finished emulsions in each of two carrier families at one room-air condition and 310.2 K. Their component calculation predicts a content for each family. Lilitari proposed estimating the mean discrepancy in one family and applying just that correction to the second. We then reversed the roles. This was retrospective. Each target had three interface-agent formulations. The unit throughout is **µmol O₂ per mL of finished fluid**. [Freire and colleagues, Tables 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fluid.2005.01.008)

| Family used for the correction | Family tested | Component-only mean absolute error | Transferred-correction mean absolute error | Range of improvement when each observation moves by one reported spread |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| C₆F₁₄ | C₁₀F₁₈ | 0.0800 | 0.0367 | +0.0267 to +0.0600 |
| C₁₀F₁₈ | C₆F₁₄ | 0.0433 | 0.0367 | −0.0467 to +0.0600 |

The benefit survives that sensitivity test in the C₆F₁₄-to-C₁₀F₁₈ direction. In the reverse direction the nominal gain is 0.0067, and its sign can change. The test holds the authors' component estimates fixed. It addresses absolute oxygen content at one condition. A pressure-response curve requires additional measurements. ## How oxygen content and pressure changed in Wong and colleagues’ preparations

Wong, DiMicco, Standaert and Dretchen used separate instruments for oxygen content and partial pressure. Sixty minutes after oxygenation stopped, an FC-80 emulsion retained **93.8%** of its initial content, compared with **64.1%** for Krebs solution. We used these fractions to forecast each fluid's terminal oxygen pressure before reading that panel of Figure 1. The criteria were an error within 5% of starting pressure and an improvement of at least 15 mmHg over holding starting pressure constant. Each observation is a group mean from three preparations. The final pressures were read from a plot with a reading allowance of ±3.65 mmHg. [Wong and colleagues, Figure 1](https://doi.org/10.1085/jgp.69.5.655)

| Fluid | Pressure predicted from oxygen remaining | Observed terminal pressure | Error of forecast | Error from holding starting pressure constant |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FC-80 emulsion | 667.3 mmHg | ≈701.4 mmHg | 34.1 mmHg | 10.2 mmHg |
| Krebs solution | 442.5 mmHg | ≈403.2 mmHg | 39.3 mmHg | 286.8 mmHg |

For the emulsion, the constant-pressure baseline was closer. For Krebs solution, the proportional forecast improved upon that baseline but missed the 34.5 mmHg tolerance. Both decisions survive the graph-reading allowance. The terminal Krebs standard error, about 42 mmHg, is larger than the forecast error. There is one held group mean per fluid.

**A separate tissue-loaded experiment.** Four preparations exposed each fluid to active diaphragm tissue. At 60 minutes the reported means ± standard error were **4.97 ± 0.56 mL O₂/dL and 477.5 ± 29.5 mmHg** for FC-80, and **0.40 ± 0.06 mL O₂/dL and 153.8 ± 26.4 mmHg** for Krebs. Oxygen consumption changed the load during this experiment. These are observations at a measured tension under load. The researchers did not impose those tensions to measure passive equilibrium uptake. Individual paired observations and covariance were unavailable. [Wong and colleagues, Figure 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2215083/) · [Replay the pressure forecasts](wong/replay_pressure.py)

## What did the hemoglobin analyzers count?

Bronkhorst-van der Helm and colleagues tested the same panel of nine blood samples containing the acellular hemoglobin carrier HBOC-201 on four laboratory instruments. They measured how far total hemoglobin and cellular hemoglobin changed from control at two added-carrier levels. At the higher level, the Siemens and Alinity instruments each showed a mean total-minus-cellular change of **1.182 g/dL**. The Sysmex instrument had a missing cellular result. Allowing any total measurement to be its unmatched partner gave a contrast of **1.229–1.269 g/dL**. These observations concern the analyzers’ hemoglobin readings. Oxygen binding and saturation would be needed to estimate carried oxygen. [Bronkhorst-van der Helm and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijlh.14146)

Across the three instruments reporting cellular hemoglobin, predicting zero change had a mean absolute error of **0.0566 g/dL**. Doubling each sample’s lower-level reading gave **0.2163–0.2536 g/dL** across all assignments permitted by the plot. Each instrument was weighted equally. The same samples were shared across instruments. The original report and reproduction code below describe how the legend, 124 plotted markers, and 14 printed group means were checked. Readers obtain the source article separately under its stated Creative Commons Attribution–NonCommercial terms. [Assay analysis](assay/REPORT.md) · [Replay from the investigators’ source PDF](assay/REPLAY.md)

## Does a chamber calibration predict oxygen under a changing inlet?

Qayyum and colleagues recorded gas oxygen at three chamber positions with an intermittent inlet command. We took each measured change in that command, applied the response shape from a separate 0-to-21% oxygen step test, and added the responses. The model used two calibration repeats at each of P1 and P2. P3 had no local step test, so we averaged the response shapes from P1 and P2. Each forecast began at the trial's observed initial gas oxygen. The model used no later gas observation from its test trial. [Qayyum and colleagues’ original workbook on Dryad](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.t1g1jwtg9)

| Chamber position | Error using the step response | Error holding starting oxygen constant | Error following the inlet immediately |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| P1 | 0.715 | 6.082 | 7.474 |
| P2 | 2.274 | 4.577 | 9.041 |
| P3 | 5.438 | 6.900 | 13.985 |

All errors are gas oxygen **percentage points**. Each entry averages the root mean square error of three recorded runs, with 43 successive times per run. The run is the unit of scoring. The 387 time points supply the shapes of nine trajectories in this apparatus. The workbook does not establish independent replication across devices. P3 combined a new position and a faster command, so the increase in error cannot be attributed to either factor individually. P1 and P2 intermittent records had been inspected in earlier work. The new forecasts were fixed before the P3 observations were scored. This test characterizes how the chamber responds to its inlet. [Run the workbook-direct replay](chamber/replay_step_to_wave.py)

To reproduce the chamber result, install NumPy and openpyxl and pass the investigators’ `Compiled_Data_PAPER_unformatted.xlsx` to `python chamber/replay_step_to_wave.py`. The script checks the source file’s SHA-256 before reading the step and intermittent sheets.

## Stirred-chamber oxygen release depends on the quantity being scored

Kakiuchi and Borden’s electrode traces allowed us to compare the shape of the released-oxygen curve with the time it took to reach 90% of its own endpoint. We used published mean traces at two stirring speeds to predict the intermediate 445-rpm trace. Interpolating the endpoints and the fraction of oxygen released gave **4.75 nmol/mL** root mean square error but reached 90% **54.2 seconds late**. Interpolating the two absolute traces directly gave **8.27 nmol/mL** error and reached 90% only **8.5 seconds late**. Each prediction was scored against the same measured mean trace. The original Figure 8 groups summarize three to four runs. The analysis was retrospective, and the authors’ individual time records were unavailable. [Kakiuchi and Borden, Figure 8](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.langmuir.4c04104)

The paper's chamber trace and endpoint bar use different concentration units. At the highest speed the sampled chamber increment of **246.15 nmol/mL** corresponds to **322.94 mg/L** when expressed per original sample volume using the combined fluid volume. The plotted endpoint was **321.67 mg/L**. The same conversion overshot the slow-speed endpoint by **8.44 mg/L**. Agreement at one speed supports the volume interpretation but does not establish a single exact conversion for all reported groups. ## Can an engineered-tissue oxygen profile predict the live-cell fraction?

Radisic and colleagues measured oxygen along the centerline of engineered cardiac tissue and the live-cell fraction across depth intervals. The Stage calibrated two equally simple models on four depth bins and reserved three for scoring. Depth alone gave **5.48 percentage points** mean absolute error in live-cell fraction, a model using the stagnant oxygen measurement gave **7.65**, and the calibration mean gave **12.58**. Depth was closer in all three reserved bins. The oxygen and viability curves came from four and three constructs respectively. The paper provides no specimen identifiers pairing the two assays. The depth bins are adjacent parts of one group profile, and the deepest calibration bin had only one contributing construct. These data cannot resolve an effect of oxygen independent of depth. [Radisic and colleagues, conference paper](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/aiche-2005/non-topical/Non%20topical/papers/306e.pdf) · [Related journal article](https://doi.org/10.1002/bit.20722) · [Viability analysis and sensitivities](viability/REPORT.md) · [Source replay](viability/replay.py)

Using the 19 displayed stagnant oxygen concentrations, a separate model assumed stationary, one-dimensional diffusion and a nonnegative oxygen sink. With the investigators’ literature value for diffusivity, integrated demand from 200 to 1600 µm corresponds to an oxygen flux difference of **14.95–294.25 nmol m⁻² s⁻¹** under the plot-reading allowance. Widening each concentration by one reported standard error expands the range to **0–863.12**. Within the widened bands, profiles that agree at every measured depth can have arbitrarily high local demand concentrated over smaller distances. This is a limit of the data and diffusion model. [Bounds and assumptions](stagnant/REPORT.md)

**Changing the external flow.** We then used the stagnant profile and the two measured surface concentrations at each externally flowed position to predict the oxygen between those surfaces. Three externally flowed constructs contributed four radial positions each. At the 13 interior depths where one cross-marked position could be followed confidently, the transferred stagnant shape had **22.50 µmol/L** root mean square error, a straight line between the surfaces gave **34.54**, and carrying the stagnant values forward unchanged gave **85.52**. The transfer still overestimated the measured interior concentrations by **20.94 µmol/L** on average.

Across all four positions, marker overlap complicates the interpretation. The registered score averages four separate position errors. At the central readings its improvement over the straight line is at least **5.029 µmol/L**, just above the five-unit target. A permitted reading and rank assignment gave **3.38**, so the registered threshold is unresolved at the figure’s resolution. A different score that pools squared errors gives a minimum improvement of **3.19 µmol/L** across the same chart allowances. This was a measured change of condition with new surface anchors, and both scoring conventions refer to the same tissue profiles. [Flow comparison, replay and frozen-coefficient provenance](flow/REPORT.md)

## What the Sharts prediction error can tell us

Our first release reported that our calibrated rule for extrapolating from each fluid's low-pressure reading had mean absolute error **0.964 mL O₂/dL**, and a training-only median gave **0.333**. The rule first subtracts the measured degassed reading, scales the excess by the ratio of the nominal equilibration pressures, then restores the degassed reading. If a fluid's oxygen content equals a constant background plus its own constant slope times pressure, both terms are already accounted for. Varying the constant background or constant slope between materials alone cannot explain the residuals under this model. In the printed means, oxygen above the degassed background per unit nominal pressure at the high condition was 88.5%, 79.5% and 75.8% of the respective low-condition values. Condition-dependent changes or measurement error could contribute. Two positive-pressure summaries per material leave the cause unresolved. [Sharts and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1139(00)81622-X) · [Frozen forecast and replay](../oxygen-measurements/two-pressure/README.md)
